Rational Root Theorem Calculator

Enter the coefficients from the highest power down to the constant term, separated by commas. Write 0 for a missing term. For 2x³ − 3x² − 8x + 12 enter 2, -3, -8, 12. Fractions and decimals are allowed and are cleared automatically.

What is the Rational Root Theorem?

Suppose a polynomial has integer coefficients, leading coefficient an and constant term a0. If a fraction p/q in lowest terms is a root, then p is a factor of the constant term a0 and q is a factor of the leading coefficient an.

This gives a finite list of candidates. You then test them, usually with synthetic division or by substituting into the polynomial. The theorem tells you where to look, but it does not say that any candidate is actually a root.

How to use it

  1. Make sure all coefficients are integers. If they are fractions, multiply the whole polynomial by a common denominator.
  2. List all factors p of the constant term and all factors q of the leading coefficient.
  3. Write every possible root ±p/q in lowest terms.
  4. Test the candidates. Each one that gives 0 is a root. Divide it out and repeat on what is left.
  5. When a quadratic is left, finish with factoring or the quadratic formula.

Worked example

Find the rational roots of 2x³ − 3x² − 8x + 12. The constant term is 12, with factors 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12. The leading coefficient is 2, with factors 1, 2. The possible rational roots are ±1, ±2, ±3, ±4, ±6, ±12, ±1/2 and ±3/2.

Testing shows f(2) = 0, f(−2) = 0 and f(3/2) = 0. So the polynomial factors as (2x − 3)(x − 2)(x + 2). The calculator above does exactly this and also shows the factored form.

Good to know

  • If the constant term is 0, factor out x first. Then 0 is a root, and the theorem is used on what remains.
  • If no candidate works, the polynomial has no rational roots. It can still have irrational or complex roots. These appear as orange dots on the graph when they are real.
  • Descartes' Rule of Signs narrows the list further by telling you how many positive and negative roots to expect. See the Descartes' rule calculator.
  • To go the other way, from roots to a polynomial, use polynomial from roots.
